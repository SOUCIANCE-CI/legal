Transfer Impact Assessment

Missing Time for Jira. Last updated 18 October 2026. Next review October 2027.

Carried out under Case C-311/18 (Schrems II) and EDPB Recommendations 01/2020, to assess whether the Standard Contractual Clauses relied on in section 7.2 of the Data Processing Addendum provide an essentially equivalent level of protection in practice.

It is published rather than provided on request, because a customer's own assessment is easier if ours is already on the table.

Step 1 — Map the transfer

The exporter is a customer organisation in the EEA. The importer is Christopher Dato, an individual developer established in Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire.

No data is sent to Côte d'Ivoire. All personal data processed by the app is read from, and written to, the Atlassian Forge infrastructure serving the customer's own Jira site. The importer operates no server, no database, no backup and no storage of any kind, and holds no credential to the customer's site.

The transfer arises solely because the importer, established in a third country, is the party that writes and deploys the code executing on that data, and may be able to see the application's logs. Under EDPB Guidelines 05/2021 that is enough: making data available to an entity in a third country is a transfer, and remote access counts even where the data never moves.

Scope of the data is set out in Annex I.B. In summary: account identifiers, display names, and a comparison between hours logged and hours expected. No special category data, no issue content, no email address.

Step 2 — Transfer tool

Standard Contractual Clauses, Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914, Module Two (controller to processor), with annexes published at scc-annexes.html. UK and Swiss variants as stated in the Addendum.

Step 3 — Law and practice of the third country

Côte d'Ivoire has a data protection statute, Law No. 2013-450 of 19 June 2013 on the protection of personal data, and a supervisory authority, the Autorité de Régulation des Télécommunications/TIC de Côte d'Ivoire (ARTCI). It also has cybercrime legislation, Law No. 2013-451, under which authorities hold investigative and production powers.

No adequacy decision exists for Côte d'Ivoire under Article 45 GDPR, so the existence of a national framework does not remove the need for safeguards. It is noted as context, not as a substitute.

The question that matters is narrower than "what are the surveillance laws". It is: could a public authority of Côte d'Ivoire compel the importer to produce the customer's personal data? That depends on what the importer holds, which is the subject of step 4.

Step 4 — Assessment of the practical risk

4.1 The decisive point

There is nothing at the importer's end to seize.

An order served on the importer by any authority, Ivorian or otherwise, could not produce the customer's data, because the importer holds:

  • no copy of it, in any form, at any time;
  • no credential allowing sign-in to the customer's Jira site;
  • no server, database, backup or export;
  • no interface or command by which Forge would disclose a customer installation's stored data to a developer.

Compliance with such an order would be factually impossible rather than legally resisted. This is a materially stronger position than encryption or key-holding arrangements, which are the measures usually proposed in response to Schrems II, because there is no ciphertext and no key: there is no data.

4.2 The residual risk, stated honestly

Two paths exist and are not dismissed.

The code. The importer writes the software that runs on the data inside Forge. A future version could be written to write personal data into the application logs, which the importer may be able to read. A version adding an outbound network permission would require a major version and explicit re-approval by the customer's administrator; a version that merely logs would not. This is the genuine access path, and it is addressed by the supplementary measures in step 5 rather than denied.

Support correspondence. A customer who emails a screenshot of the report sends personal data to Côte d'Ivoire in the ordinary sense. This is the one place where data really does arrive at the importer's end. It is addressed by asking customers not to do it, by using the data only to resolve the request, and by deleting it on closure.

4.3 Likelihood

The importer has received no request from any public authority for access to customer data, in Côte d'Ivoire or elsewhere. The data concerned is working-time administration of private-sector employees, of no evident interest to law enforcement or national security. The customer base consists of businesses using Jira. No category of data subject at heightened risk is involved.

Step 5 — Supplementary measures

Contractual and organisational measures, each aimed at the residual risk identified in step 4.2 rather than at a generic checklist. All are binding on the importer through section 7.3 of the Addendum.

MeasureRisk addressed
No local copy of personal data will be downloaded, exported or replicated out of Atlassian infrastructure, by any means, including for debugging. Removes the possibility of a seizable copy coming into existence.
No outbound network permission will be introduced without updating the Addendum, and is in any event subject to the customer's administrator approval. Keeps the architectural guarantee in place.
No personal data in logs. Logging is restricted to aggregate counts and non-personal diagnostics, re-verified at each release. Closes the code path identified in 4.2.
Public authority requests handled under Clause 15: prompt notification where lawful, challenge where grounds exist, minimum disclosure if compelled. Procedural safeguard for the unlikely case.
Annual transparency reporting on request, including nil returns. Lets the customer verify the assumption in 4.3 rather than take it on trust.
Customers are asked not to send screenshots or exports containing employee names to support; such material is used only to resolve the request and deleted on closure. Addresses the support correspondence path.

Step 6 — Conclusion and re-evaluation

With the Standard Contractual Clauses and the supplementary measures above, the transfer achieves a level of protection essentially equivalent to that guaranteed within the EEA. The determining factor is not a contractual promise but a structural one: the importer holds no copy of the data and no means of obtaining one, so the government access risk that Schrems II is concerned with has almost no surface to act on.

This assessment is reviewed at least annually, and immediately on any of the following: a change to the application's egress or write permissions; a change to the storage keys or to logging; the engagement of a new sub-processor; a material change to Ivorian law relevant to access by public authorities; or receipt of any request from a public authority for customer data.
